What's the HOS?

HOS 2 (Hands-On Skill) is this module's main build and 10% of your course grade. It has four stages across the two weeks: Create / Scaffold and Evaluate in Week 3, then Analyze and Understand & Refine in Week 4. All four go in one GitHub repo, and you submit that repo's link once, in Brightspace, at the end of Week 4. The Muddiest Point, Concept Debate, Application Challenge and Knowledge Check are graded separately.

Your HOS 1 agent can reason and call tools, but it knows nothing about your business. Ask it how long Northwind Outfitters gives customers to return a tent and it will answer anyway — with a typical return window it has seen somewhere, stated confidently. This HOS fixes that by giving the model the right few paragraphs of your own documents at the moment it answers. That's Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): retrieve first, then generate.

Week 3 gets the pipeline working (chunk, embed, store, retrieve, inject) and tests it deliberately, including the bug where adding RAG quietly breaks the tool calling you built in HOS 1. Week 4 is about understanding why retrieval works: why the query and the documents are embedded differently, and whether you can add knowledge to the system by hand.

Anchoring analogy

HOS 1's consultant is still on the phone, and still can't see your filing cabinet. RAG gives you a very fast assistant who, the moment a question comes in, pulls the three most relevant pages out of the cabinet and slides them across the desk before the consultant answers. The consultant's skill doesn't change; what changes is what's on the desk. If the assistant pulls the wrong pages, the consultant gives a confident answer from the wrong pages — which is why most RAG bugs are retrieval bugs, not model bugs.

Learning objectives

By the end of the two weeks you will be able to:

  1. Explain what an embedding is and why similar meanings produce nearby vectors, even with different wording. O2
  2. Justify a chunking choice (size and overlap) and describe what goes wrong when chunks are too big or too small. O2
  3. Design and implement a RAG pipeline: chunk → embed → store in a vector database → retrieve top-k → inject as context. O2
  4. Explain why documents and queries use different embedding task types, and what quietly degrades if they don't. O2
  5. Test whether retrieval is actually helping, including when the documents don't cover the question and when a tool should still be called. O2

Part 1 — Why the model needs your context ≈ 10 min

Watch first6:35 · IBM Technology

Watch for the two problems retrieval fixes (no source for the answer, and knowledge that's out of date) and for the split between the part that finds text and the part that writes the answer. Open on YouTube (human captions).

An LLM's knowledge is fixed at training time and general by design. It has never seen Northwind Outfitters' return policy, and it can't tell you that it hasn't: it predicts a likely-sounding answer either way. HOS 1 gave the model tools for things it can't compute; HOS 2 gives it context for things it can't know.

There are three ways to get your knowledge into an answer, and it's worth knowing why this course picks the third:

Retrain (fine-tune) the modelPaste every document into every requestRetrieve the relevant pieces (RAG)
Update a policyRetrain: slow, needs data and moneyEdit the file: instantRe-ingest one file: instant
Cost per questionLowHigh: every token is paid for, every timeLow: a few chunks per question
Can it cite a source?NoYes, but it's buried in a huge promptYes: you know exactly which chunks it saw
Scales to 1,000 documents?Yes, with retrainingNo: the context window runs outYes

Pasting everything works for five short documents and stops working quickly. Every token you send costs time and money, the context window has a limit, and a model handed fifty pages answers worse than one handed the right three paragraphs. So the real task is selection: deciding, per question, which few pieces of text the model should see. That's the context engineering you met in HOS 1, now done automatically by code.

That's what Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) means. Retrieval: find the relevant text. Augmented: add it to the request. Generation: the model writes the answer using it. The model itself doesn't change at all; only what you send it does.

Worked example

A customer asks, "Can I send back a tent I bought three weeks ago?" Without retrieval, the model says something like "Most retailers allow 14 to 30 days." With retrieval, the backend first finds the chunk from return-policy.md ("returns within 30 days of delivery … unused and in its original packaging") and puts it in the system instruction. The model's answer is now Northwind's actual policy, including the packaging condition that a generic answer would have skipped. And because you know which chunk it saw, you can check the answer against it.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Trusting a fluent answer. "It sounded right" is not evidence the model read your documents. Compare the answer with the retrieved chunks.
  • Expecting retrieval to answer questions the documents don't cover. If Northwind's docs never mention price matching, RAG can't help; the app should say it doesn't know.
  • Thinking RAG changes the model. Nothing is trained. Delete the retrieved context and the model is exactly as ignorant as before.
Quick check

Northwind changes its return window from 30 to 45 days. With RAG, what do you have to do so the assistant gives the new answer? With fine-tuning, what would you have to do?

Part 2 — Embeddings: turning meaning into numbers ≈ 15 min

Watch first8:38 · IBM Technology

The video embeds single words; Gemini embeds whole passages, but the idea is the same. Watch for "similar meaning, nearby vectors," which is the only property retrieval relies on. Open on YouTube (human captions).

Go deeper (optional): 3Blue1Brown, Transformers, the tech behind LLMs (27:14, human captions). Its opening sections show embeddings as directions in space, with memorable pictures.

An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that represents the meaning of a piece of text. Gemini's gemini-embedding-001 model turns any text into such a vector; the HOS 2 code asks for 1,024 numbers per text. You never read those numbers yourself. What matters is that texts with similar meaning get vectors that point in similar directions.

"Point in similar directions" has a precise meaning. Picture each vector as an arrow. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two arrows: close to 1 means they point the same way ("about the same thing"), close to 0 means unrelated. A two-number example makes it concrete:

Text (toy 2-number embedding)Compared with "return policy" [3, 1]Why
"sending an item back" [6, 2]1.00, same directionTwice as long, but the same angle: same meaning
"tent sizes" [2, 3]0.79Partly related (both are about products)
"store hours" [−1, 3]0.00, at right anglesUnrelated

Real embeddings have 1,024 numbers instead of 2, but the comparison works exactly the same way. Chroma ranks chunks by distance between vectors; for this course, read "closest" as "most similar in meaning."

This is why RAG beats keyword search. A customer who types "sending an item back" never uses the word "return," but its embedding still lands near the return-policy chunk.

"Can I send back a tent?" ↔ return-policy chunkhigh "Can I send back a tent?" ↔ product-tents chunkmedium "Can I send back a tent?" ↔ shipping chunklow

Illustrative: the tent guide shares the word "tent," but the return policy shares the meaning.

Here is the embedding call from the HOS 2 build, trimmed to the parts that matter:

result = client.models.embed_content(
    model="gemini-embedding-001",
    contents=query,
    config=types.EmbedContentConfig(
        task_type="RETRIEVAL_QUERY",      # a question; chunks use RETRIEVAL_DOCUMENT
        output_dimensionality=EMBED_DIM,  # 1024, and identical in ingest.py
    ),
)
q_vec = result.embeddings[0].values      # the list of 1,024 numbers

Two settings in it matter. First, Gemini lets you say what a text is for: documents are embedded with task_type="RETRIEVAL_DOCUMENT" and questions with task_type="RETRIEVAL_QUERY". Why that helps is your Week 4 Analyze question; for now, notice that nothing crashes if you get it wrong. Second, EMBED_DIM, the vector length, must be identical at ingest time and question time, or the comparison is meaningless.

Worked example

A student changes EMBED_DIM to 768 in retrieval.py to "make it faster" but doesn't re-run ingest.py. The stored chunks still have 1,024 numbers each; the question now has 768. Chroma refuses to compare vectors of different lengths and the request fails. The fix isn't in the query code: re-ingest with the same setting, or change it back. Settings that shape vectors belong in one place, used by both scripts.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Treating similarity as truth. The closest chunk is the most related text, not necessarily the right answer. A chunk about tent returns can be close to a question about tent warranties.
  • Changing the embedding model or size without re-ingesting. Vectors from different settings can't be compared.
  • Swapping the task types. Embedding chunks as queries (or the reverse) doesn't error; results just get quietly worse.
Quick check

If you embed the question "What's your refund window?" and the only document chunk says "returns within 30 days," would keyword search find it? Would embeddings? Why?

Part 3 — Chunking and the RAG pipeline ≈ 15 min

Watch first8:11 · IBM Technology

Chroma, which you'll use, is a small vector database that stores its data in a local folder. Watch for what it stores (vectors plus the original text) and what it's fast at (finding the nearest vectors to a query). Open on YouTube (human captions).

You don't embed whole documents. A document covering shipping times and customs fees produces one vector that's a blurry average of both, a mediocre match for either question. So you chunk first: split each document into focused pieces, and overlap neighboring chunks so a key sentence isn't cut in half. The HOS 2 code does the simplest version, by word count:

def chunk_text(text, chunk_size=400, overlap=50):
    words = text.split()
    chunks, start = [], 0
    while start < len(words):
        chunks.append(" ".join(words[start:start + chunk_size]))
        start += chunk_size - overlap   # step back 50 words: the overlap
    return chunks

A 1,000-word document becomes three chunks: words 1–400, 351–750 and 701–1,000. A sentence that falls around word 400 appears whole in at least one of them. Northwind's five sample documents are short (80 to 100 words each), so at these settings each one is a single chunk.

Chunk size is a trade-off you'll tune in the simulator. Too big, and each chunk mixes topics, so its vector is vague and the model gets paragraphs it doesn't need. Too small, and a chunk loses the sentence that gives it meaning ("…within 30 days" without saying of what). The number of chunks you retrieve, top-k, is the same kind of trade-off: too few and the answer may be missing; too many and you're back to pasting everything.

Notice that ingest runs once and retrieval runs on every question. If you edit a document and don't re-run ingest, the app keeps answering from the old version, because Chroma still holds the old chunks.

Notice the last box too: your tools are still there. The most common HOS 2 bug is a system instruction like "only answer from the context; otherwise say you don't know." Applied broadly, it makes the model refuse to call calculate, because arithmetic isn't in the context either. Nothing errors; tool calls just stop happening. Scope the instruction to content questions and say tools are exempt.

Worked example

With the starter's settings, the five short sample documents become five chunks, and retrieve(k=5) returns all of them, so every question sees every document. That's fine for learning the plumbing, and it hides the selection problem that appears once Northwind has 500 documents. To see selection happen, shrink chunks to about 20 words and set top-k to 1. Now shipping.md splits into several pieces, and "Canadian orders may be subject to customs fees…" becomes its own chunk. Ask "Do Canadian orders pay customs fees?" and that chunk should rank first. Ask "How long does shipping to Canada take?" and the top chunk is likely the standard-shipping one: "5-7 business days," with nothing about customs. The answer is grounded but incomplete, and the cause is the retrieval settings, not the model.

⚠ Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to re-ingest after changing documents or chunk settings. The app answers from stale chunks and nothing tells you.
  • An over-broad "only use the context" instruction that silently disables the HOS 1 tools.
  • Blaming the model first. When an answer is wrong, look at what was retrieved before changing the prompt. Most RAG bugs are retrieval bugs.
Quick check

A 900-word document goes through chunk_text with the default settings. How many chunks come out, and which words does each one start at?

New words this week (4)
Embedding & cosine similarity
A vector representing a text's meaning, and the score (roughly 0 to 1) for how closely two vectors point the same way.
Chunking & overlap
Splitting documents into focused pieces before embedding them, with a few words shared between neighbors so nothing important is cut in half.
Vector database (Chroma)
A store built to hold embeddings and quickly return the ones nearest a query. Chroma writes to a local folder, like SQLite.
RAG & top-k
Retrieval-Augmented Generation: fetch the k most similar chunks, then let the model answer using them.

    You'll argue the side your debate group is assigned, then argue the other one, then drop advocacy and synthesize.

    Your side this HOS

Before posting Stage 1, count the posts on each side and join the side with fewer; if they're tied, take Side A. How sides are assigned →

    Side A — Refuse when retrieval comes up emptyGroup 2 this HOS

    If no chunk clears the similarity threshold, say so and point to a human or a source.

    • A made-up policy ("yes, we gift wrap") creates a promise the business has to honor or retract.
    • Users can't tell a grounded answer from a general one unless the system is strict.
    • Refusals are testable: an uncovered question should always produce the same safe answer.

    Side B — Answer from general knowledge, with a caveatGroup 1 this HOS

    Give the best general answer, clearly labeled as not from company documents.

    • "I don't know" to every uncovered question makes the assistant feel broken.
    • Many questions ("how do I waterproof a tent?") are general, and the model's knowledge is genuinely useful.
    • A clear caveat keeps the user informed without leaving them with nothing.

    Application Challenge — measure someone else's RAG pipeline

    Measure & improve · 60–90 min

    A campus IT help-desk team built the pipeline below to answer student questions from their knowledge base. It runs without errors, and nobody knows how often it answers from the wrong passage. In the challenge you get a runnable copy, six articles, and 20 questions with known answers: measure how often it finds the answer, beat it with your own chunking, and decide when the bot should say "I don't know". The spec, starter code, and rubric are in the AC 2 guide.

    Warm up first: read the production code and predict what will go wrong. The challenge then measures it.

    # helpdesk_rag.py  (someone else's code)
kb = chromadb.PersistentClient(path="./kb_db")
col = kb.get_or_create_collection("articles")

def ingest(article_id, text):
    for i in range(0, len(text), 500):               # split every 500 characters
        piece = text[i:i+500]
        vec = embed(piece, task_type="RETRIEVAL_QUERY")
        col.add(ids=[f"{article_id}-{i}"], embeddings=[vec],
                documents=[piece], metadatas=[{"source": article_id}])

def answer(question):
    q = embed(question, task_type="RETRIEVAL_QUERY")
    hits = col.query(query_embeddings=[q], n_results=1)
    context = hits["documents"][0][0]
    return gemini(f"{question}\n\nAnswer using this: {context}")

    Warm-up: classify each observation

    Is each one something the code gets right, something that will quietly underperform, or a crash risk? Then reveal the reasoning.

    What to submit
    • evaluate.py and tests/test_my_rules.py
    • The output of pytest, all tests passing (including yours)
    • REPORT.md: the output of python run_eval.py
    • DECISIONS.md: your threshold, and why each miss happens

    Grading: Application — genuinely applied to the new pipeline, not a restatement of your own build (10%) · Analysis — break the problem into its parts (20%) · Evaluation — justify your call against clear criteria (30%) · Design — your own original solution (40%).
